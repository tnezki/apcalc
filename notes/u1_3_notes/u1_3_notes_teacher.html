Algebraic Limits
Algebraic limits are solved by recognizing the form first and then choosing a method that preserves nearby behavior.

Mathematical idea: The expression’s form determines whether direct substitution, factoring, rationalization, a trigonometric limit, or squeeze reasoning is appropriate.

You will move from direct substitution to indeterminate forms, then compare several techniques for resolving limits efficiently and justifiably.

Reading 1: Start with the Form

Direct substitution is the first method to try for many algebraic limits. Substitute the target input into the expression and simplify. If the result is an ordinary finite number and the expression is continuous there, the limit is usually settled immediately.

This works for polynomials and for many rational, radical, exponential, and trigonometric expressions at points where they are defined and continuous. The key is not blind substitution but recognizing when continuity supports the shortcut.

The limit laws formalize how limits interact with arithmetic. If $f(x)\to L$ and $g(x)\to M$, then sums, differences, constant multiples, and products approach the corresponding combinations of $L$ and $M$. Quotients behave similarly when the limiting denominator is not zero.

Limit laws let a complicated expression be decomposed into familiar pieces. For example, if $\lim f(x)=5$ and $\lim g(x)=-1$, then $\lim[2f(x)+3g(x)]$ is computed from the known limiting values rather than by creating a new table or graph.

Substitution also functions as a diagnostic. A result such as $0/0$ is an indeterminate form, which means the visible expression does not yet determine the limit. It is a signal to transform the expression or use a theorem, not a final answer.

A good algebraic-limit solution therefore begins with two questions: what happens under substitution, and what structural feature of the expression tells me what to do next?

MethodFirst Check: Substitute

Begin by substituting the target input. A finite ordinary value often finishes the problem. An indeterminate form such as $0/0$ means the expression needs to be transformed before the limit can be evaluated.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why is $0/0$ a signal rather than an answer?
  2. What does a finite direct-substitution result tell you when the expression is continuous?

Expected discussion: 1. Different nearby behaviors can produce the same indeterminate form, so more structure is needed.<br/>2. The limit equals that finite substituted value.

Example 1: Direct Substitution and Limit Laws

Use direct substitution and limit laws where they apply.

  1. Evaluate $\lim_{x\to-3}(3x^2+2x-3)$.
  2. Given $\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=5$ and $\lim_{x\to2}g(x)=-1$, evaluate $\lim_{x\to2}[2f(x)+3g(x)]$.
  3. State why direct substitution is valid for the polynomial in part (a).

Teacher answer:

  1. $18$.
  2. $2(5)+3(-1)=7$.
  3. Polynomials are continuous, so their limits can be evaluated by direct substitution.

You Try It 1: Direct Substitution and Limit Laws

  2. Given $\lim_{x\to-1}p(x)=4$ and $\lim_{x\to-1}q(x)=2$, evaluate $\lim_{x\to-1}[3p(x)-q(x)]$.

Teacher answer:

Reading 2: Equivalent Nearby Expressions

Factoring is useful when substitution produces $0/0$ and the numerator and denominator share a factor. After factoring, a common factor can often be canceled for every nearby input except the target itself.

The cancellation is legitimate for a limit because limits depend on nearby values. The original expression and the simplified expression do not need to agree at the target point; they need to agree throughout a punctured neighborhood around it.

For example, $\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ becomes $x+3$ for $x\ne3$. The original expression has a hole at 3, but the simplified expression reveals that nearby outputs approach 6. This is a precise algebraic version of the graphical “hole” idea from earlier sections.

Radical expressions often require a different structural move. A conjugate changes the sign between two terms, such as $\sqrt{x}-3$ becoming $\sqrt{x}+3$. Multiplying by the conjugate uses a difference-of-squares identity to replace a radical difference with an algebraic factor that can cancel.

Factoring and conjugates have the same conceptual purpose: create an equivalent expression that is easier to evaluate near the target. The algebra is different, but the limit logic is identical.

Always record the restriction that the cancellation is for nearby inputs, not a claim that the original formula suddenly becomes defined at the missing point.

Important ConceptEquivalent Near the Target

For limits, two expressions may be used interchangeably when they agree for all inputs sufficiently close to the target except possibly at the target itself. This is why canceling a common factor after factoring can reveal a removable hole.

Example 2: Factoring Away an Indeterminate Form

Use factoring to remove a common factor that creates an indeterminate form.

  1. Evaluate $\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$.
  2. Evaluate $\lim_{x\to-2}\frac{x^2+5x+6}{x+2}$.
  3. Explain why canceling the common factor is legitimate for a limit even though the original expressions are undefined at the target input.

Teacher answer:

  1. $\lim_{x\to3}(x+3)=6$.
  2. $\lim_{x\to-2}(x+3)=1$.
  3. A limit depends on nearby values. The original and simplified expressions agree for all nearby inputs except the target point.

You Try It 2: Factoring Away an Indeterminate Form

Factor, simplify, and evaluate each limit.

  1. $\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$.
  2. $\lim_{x\to4}\frac{x^2-7x+12}{x-4}$.
  3. For one part, identify the factor whose cancellation resolves the $0/0$ form.

Teacher answer:

  1. $2$ after canceling $x-1$.
  2. $1$ after factoring $(x-3)(x-4)$ and canceling $x-4$.
  3. Part (a): $x-1$; part (b): $x-4$. Either correct identification earns the explanation.

Reading 3: Trig Limits and Squeeze Reasoning

Some limits are controlled by standard trigonometric behavior. The foundational relationship $\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\sin u}{u}=1$ allows expressions such as $\frac{\sin(5x)}{x}$ to be rewritten so the standard form appears.

The goal is not to memorize every possible coefficient pattern. Rewrite the expression so the same quantity appears inside the sine and in its denominator. Then separate any constant factor that remains.

The Squeeze Theorem handles a different situation: a function is trapped between two others whose limits are known and equal. If $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ near the target and both outer functions approach $L$, then the middle function must also approach $L$.

The theorem depends on both ingredients. The ordering must hold in a neighborhood of the target, and the two bounding limits must agree. If the bounds approach different values, the theorem does not force a unique limit for the middle function.

Squeeze reasoning is especially powerful when a function oscillates or is difficult to simplify directly but its magnitude can be bounded by something small. It converts an inequality into a limit conclusion.

Conjugates, special trigonometric limits, and squeeze arguments look different on paper, but they share one strategic idea: transform the original problem into a form whose nearby behavior is already understood.

That is why method selection matters. Rather than applying every tool mechanically, inspect the form and choose the technique that exposes the limiting behavior most directly.

FormulaConjugate Pattern

Use $(A-B)(A+B)=A^2-B^2$ to eliminate a radical difference. For example:

$$\frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}+3}{\sqrt{x}+3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+3},\quad x\ne9.$$

Stop and Discuss

  1. What condition must be true about both outside functions before the Squeeze Theorem determines the middle limit?
  2. How are factoring and conjugates conceptually similar in a limit problem?

Expected discussion: 1. Both outside functions must approach the same limit while bounding the middle function nearby.<br/>2. Both create an equivalent nearby expression that exposes known limiting behavior.

Example 3: Conjugates, Trig, and Squeeze

Different forms call for different algebraic or theorem-based tools.

  1. Evaluate $\lim_{x\to9}\frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9}$ using a conjugate.
  2. Evaluate $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(5x)}{x}$ using a standard trigonometric limit.
  3. If $-x^2\le h(x)\le x^2$ for $x$ near 0, determine $\lim_{x\to0}h(x)$.

Teacher answer:

  1. Multiply by the conjugate to obtain $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+3}$, so the limit is $\frac16$.
  2. $5$ because $\frac{\sin(5x)}{x}=5\frac{\sin(5x)}{5x}$.
  3. $0$ by the Squeeze Theorem because both bounding functions approach 0.

You Try It 3: Conjugates, Trig, and Squeeze

Choose and carry out an appropriate method for each limit.

  1. $\lim_{x\to16}\frac{\sqrt{x}-4}{x-16}$.
  2. $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(3x)}{x}$.
  3. If $2-x^2\le k(x)\le2+x^2$ near 0, determine $\lim_{x\to0}k(x)$.

Teacher answer:

  1. $\frac18$ after multiplying by the conjugate.
  2. $3$.
  3. $2$ by the Squeeze Theorem.

TheoremSqueeze Theorem

If $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ near $x=a$ and

$$\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L=\lim_{x\to a}h(x),$$

then $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$.

Summary

  • Try direct substitution first because it immediately identifies many limits and reveals indeterminate forms when more work is needed.
  • An indeterminate form such as $0/0$ is a signal to transform the expression, not an answer.
  • Factoring and conjugates create equivalent expressions for nearby inputs, which is exactly what a limit needs.
  • Standard trigonometric limits and the Squeeze Theorem provide additional tools when algebra alone does not settle the form.
  • Strong limit work includes selecting and justifying a method, not merely carrying out algebra.
