Section Outline

Section 5.1: GCF and Structure

Learning Target: Students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct equivalent polynomial expressions by factoring out a greatest common factor.
  • I can apply structure to rewrite polynomial expressions in a more useful form.
  • I can determine whether a rewritten expression is reasonable by checking common factors and equivalent products.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • greatest common factor
  • factor
  • coefficient
  • monomial
  • distributive property

Key Representations

  • Polynomial expressions in expanded form
  • Factored expressions with greatest common factors
  • Area models for common-factor structure
  • Tables of term coefficients and variable powers
  • Verbal contexts involving shared quantities

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Factoring out a common factor that is not greatest and stopping too early.
  • Forgetting to divide every term by the factor.
  • Losing a negative sign when a negative GCF is useful.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Use area or array models to represent common factors.
  • Rewrite expressions from contexts where quantities share a common part.
  • Build equivalent expressions to simplify repeated calculations.
  • Check factored expressions by multiplying back to the original expression.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

Review: Add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions.

Mastery: Solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types; also solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

Section 5.2: Factoring Trinomials

Learning Target: Students will factor quadratic trinomials using patterns, products, sums, and structure.

I Can Statements

  • I can build factored forms of quadratic trinomials using product-sum relationships.
  • I can use patterns in coefficients and constants to factor quadratic trinomials.
  • I can predict missing factors or terms by extending product-sum reasoning.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • trinomial
  • factor pair
  • product
  • sum
  • factored form
  • quadratic

Key Representations

  • Standard-form quadratic trinomials
  • Factored binomial products
  • Product-sum tables
  • Algebra tiles or area models
  • Partial-factor work showing structure

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Choosing factor pairs that have the correct product but wrong sum.
  • Failing to verify factors by multiplication.
  • Assuming every trinomial factors over the integers.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent trinomials with area models before writing factors.
  • Build product-sum tables to organize factor choices.
  • Connect binomial multiplication to the reverse process of factoring.
  • Use structure to choose efficient factoring strategies.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will factor quadratic trinomials using patterns, products, sums, and structure.

Review: Add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions; also connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features.

Mastery: Solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

Section 5.3: Special Factoring Patterns

Learning Target: Students will recognize and factor special polynomial patterns.

I Can Statements

  • I can design a strategy for factoring special polynomial patterns.
  • I can apply difference-of-squares and perfect-square-trinomial patterns.
  • I can assess whether a polynomial fits a special factoring pattern before rewriting it.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • difference of squares
  • perfect-square trinomial
  • factored form
  • square

Key Representations

  • Difference-of-squares expressions
  • Perfect-square trinomials
  • Squared binomial forms
  • Pattern templates using variables and coefficients
  • Visual square and rectangle models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Treating a sum of squares as a difference of squares.
  • Recognizing the outer squares but not checking the middle term of a perfect-square trinomial.
  • Stopping before all factors are fully factored.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Create examples and nonexamples of special factoring patterns.
  • Use geometric models to represent perfect squares and differences of squares.
  • Connect pattern recognition to efficient rewriting.
  • Check special pattern factorizations by expanding.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will recognize and factor special polynomial patterns.

Review: Add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions.

Mastery: Solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

Section 5.4: Factored Form and Zeros

Learning Target: Students will connect factors, zeros, intercepts, and graphs of quadratic functions.

I Can Statements

  • I can formulate quadratic functions in factored form from zeros or intercepts.
  • I can use factored form to predict zeros and x-intercepts of quadratic graphs.
  • I can extend connections among factors, zeros, intercepts, and graph behavior.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • zero-product property
  • zero
  • root
  • solution
  • $x$-intercept
  • factor

Key Representations

  • Factored quadratic functions
  • Zeros and x-intercepts
  • Quadratic graphs
  • Input-output tables near intercepts
  • Context statements involving break-even or height reaching zero

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Setting factors equal to the constant term instead of zero.
  • Confusing zeros with $y$-intercepts.
  • Reading a factor $(x-r)$ as a zero of $-r$ instead of $r$.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Write factored functions from graph intercepts.
  • Model situations where zeros represent meaningful values.
  • Use zeros to sketch or interpret quadratic graphs.
  • Connect algebraic factors to graphical crossings.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will connect factors, zeros, intercepts, and graphs of quadratic functions.

Review: Connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features.

Mastery: Solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types; also create and solve systems that model real-world situations.

Section 5.5: Comparing Quadratic Forms

Learning Target: Students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct equivalent quadratic forms to reveal different key features.
  • I can interpret standard, vertex, and factored forms to read key features of quadratic functions.
  • I can critique which quadratic form best supports a given question or context.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • standard form
  • vertex form
  • factored form
  • vertex
  • zeros
  • axis of symmetry

Key Representations

  • Standard form
  • Vertex form
  • Factored form
  • Quadratic graphs with key features labeled
  • Contexts involving maximums, minimums, intercepts, and starting values

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Assuming one quadratic form is always best for every question.
  • Reading zeros directly from standard form without solving or rewriting.
  • Confusing the vertex information revealed by vertex form with intercept information.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Choose the most useful form for a context-based question.
  • Rewrite quadratics to reveal zeros, vertex, or y-intercept.
  • Connect forms to graph features and contextual meaning.
  • Critique form choices in student solution strategies.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

Review: Graph and write translated functions using key points and key features; Analyze and graph transformations involving stretches, compressions, and reflections; and connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features.

Mastery: Solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types; Create and solve systems that model real-world situations; and analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities.

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Using polynomial structure to rewrite expressions efficiently
  • Connecting factored expressions to products, areas, and zeros
  • Representing quadratic relationships through multiple equivalent forms
  • Interpreting zeros, intercepts, and key features in graphs and contexts
  • Selecting forms strategically based on the information a problem asks for

Mastery Goals

U5-MG01

Solve systems of linear equations using graphs, elimination, and substitution, and interpret solution types.

Supporting learning / prerequisites

  • I can construct graphical representations of systems and connect intersection points to solutions.
  • I can use elimination to solve systems and interpret the solution type.
  • I can determine whether a system has one solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions from algebraic and graphical evidence.
  • I can formulate a substitution plan when one equation is already written in an efficient form.
  • I can apply substitution to solve systems and interpret the ordered-pair solution.

Key representations

  • Graphs of two linear equations
  • Tables of paired values
  • Standard form equations
  • Intersection points
  • Solution-type statements
  • Slope-intercept form equations
  • Equations solved for one variable

Recurring practices

  • connect algebraic solutions to graphical intersections or feasible regions
  • preserve equivalence while manipulating equations and inequalities
  • interpret solution types and constraints in context

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Independently demonstrates the goal with accurate mathematics, appropriate representation use, and an interpretation or justification that is consistent with the original task or context.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions

  • Treating elimination as a procedure without preserving equivalent equations.
  • Misinterpreting parallel or identical lines as having one solution.
  • Failing to connect an algebraic solution to the graph intersection.
  • Substituting into the same equation instead of the other equation.
U5-MG02

Create and solve systems that model real-world situations and interpret the intersection in context.

Supporting learning / prerequisites

  • I can build a system of equations that represents two constraints in a situation.
  • I can predict the meaning of a solution before solving from the context and variables.
  • I can design a solution pathway that connects equations, calculations, and a contextual conclusion.

Key representations

  • Verbal situations
  • Variable definitions
  • Systems of equations
  • Graphs or tables for checking
  • Contextual conclusions

Recurring practices

  • connect algebraic solutions to graphical intersections or feasible regions
  • preserve equivalence while manipulating equations and inequalities
  • interpret solution types and constraints in context

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Independently demonstrates the goal with accurate mathematics, appropriate representation use, and an interpretation or justification that is consistent with the original task or context.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions

  • Using the same variable meaning inconsistently across equations.
  • Writing two equations that do not represent the same contextual quantities.
  • Accepting an intersection that violates a real-world restriction.
U5-MG03

Graph linear inequalities accurately and interpret boundary and region information.

Supporting learning / prerequisites

  • I can construct a boundary line and shaded region for a linear inequality.
  • I can use test points to interpret which region satisfies an inequality.
  • I can critique a graph of a linear inequality using the boundary, shading, and solution region.

Key representations

  • Linear inequalities
  • Solid and dashed boundary lines
  • Shaded half-planes
  • Test points
  • Solution-region statements

Recurring practices

  • connect algebraic solutions to graphical intersections or feasible regions
  • preserve equivalence while manipulating equations and inequalities
  • interpret solution types and constraints in context

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Independently demonstrates the goal with accurate mathematics, appropriate representation use, and an interpretation or justification that is consistent with the original task or context.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions

  • Using a solid boundary when the boundary is excluded, or dashed when included.
  • Shading the wrong half-plane.
  • Treating a boundary line as the entire solution set.
U5-MG04

Analyze systems of linear inequalities as intersecting constraints and interpret feasible solution regions.

Supporting learning / prerequisites

  • I can formulate a system of inequalities that represents multiple constraints.
  • I can extend graphing strategies to represent overlapping solution regions.
  • I can apply feasible-region reasoning to interpret possible solutions in context.

Key representations

  • Systems of inequalities
  • Multiple boundary lines
  • Overlapping shaded regions
  • Feasible points
  • Constraint-based contexts

Recurring practices

  • connect algebraic solutions to graphical intersections or feasible regions
  • preserve equivalence while manipulating equations and inequalities
  • interpret solution types and constraints in context

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Independently demonstrates the goal with accurate mathematics, appropriate representation use, and an interpretation or justification that is consistent with the original task or context.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions

  • Shading each inequality correctly but not identifying the overlap as the system solution.
  • Ignoring contextual constraints such as nonnegative quantities.
  • Assuming every pair of inequalities has a feasible region.
