Chase the Tangent

Teacher guidance for the interactive secant-to-tangent investigation. The legacy filename is retained only so existing course links continue to work.

PurposePreview instantaneous rate of change through moving secant lines.
Student modePairs are ideal; each student should record their own reasoning.
TimeAbout 30–40 minutes, depending on discussion.
Scope guard: This is a conceptual preview, not the formal derivative definition lesson. Students should experience the need for “getting closer to 0” before you name limits, derivatives, or derivative rules.

Learning Job

Students use a movable second point B=(a+h,f(a+h)) to see how a secant line changes as h becomes small. They then use the apparent limiting slope to estimate a tangent line and write that line with point-slope form.

y = m(x - x1) + y1

The investigation deliberately shows the current secant slope but does not reveal a tangent slope or completed tangent-line equation.

Before Class

  • Open the student investigation once on the same type of device students will use and confirm the sliders respond.
  • Students need no Desmos account or external graphing page; the graphs and sliders are built into the HTML.
  • Plan to pause briefly after Screens 1–3 and again after Screen 7. Those are the strongest discussion points.
  • If students work in pairs, have them discuss while moving sliders but require each student to write their own responses.

Mathematics at a Glance

For the quadratic, the secant slope simplifies to 2+h when h≠0. For the cubic, the secant slope is 3.2+2.1h+0.35h². These are useful teacher checks, not formulas to give students during the investigation.

Screen-by-Screen Facilitation

Screen 1

Quadratic — What does h control?

Student job: Predict first, then move h from both positive and negative values toward 0.

Listen for: B moves toward A; the secant line settles toward one direction; secant slopes appear to settle near 2.

Teacher move: Ask, “What does the sign of h change? What does the size of |h| change?”

Do not reveal yet: “The derivative is 2.” Let students describe the observed approach first.
Screen 2

Cubic — Does the same idea survive?

Student job: Repeat the experiment on a graph with changing curvature and a target point away from the axes.

Expected observation: Secant slopes from both sides approach about 3.2.

Teacher move: Ask students to compare the behavior to Screen 1 rather than focus only on getting a decimal.

Screen 3

Sine — What is h doing now?

Student job: Use the same h-process on a non-polynomial curve.

Expected observation: The secant slope appears to approach about 0.707 from either side.

Teacher move: Emphasize that the procedure is the same even though the function family changed.

Key generalization: The useful idea is not “quadratics have tangent lines.” It is that nearby secant slopes can reveal a local rate of change on very different smooth curves.
Screen 4

Why did anyone care about tangent lines?

Student job: Connect the mathematical need to motion, astronomy, growth, and optimization.

Teacher move: Keep this brief. The goal is motivation: curved phenomena change from moment to moment, so average change over an interval is not enough when the question is “what is happening right now?”

Good debrief question: “What did the h-experiment give us that one large secant interval could not?”

Screen 5

Build a tangent-line estimate — quadratic

Student job: Use their secant evidence to choose a candidate tangent slope m, then write the line through A=(1,1) with point-slope form.

y = m(x - x1) + y1

Expected estimate: m≈2, so a strong equation is y=2(x-1)+1.

Teacher move: If students struggle, point to the fixed point and ask, “Which two numbers belong in x₁,y₁?” Avoid supplying m.

Important: The slider only changes the candidate line. Students must write the equation themselves; the page should not auto-complete it for them.
Screen 6

Use point-slope form again — cubic

Student job: Repeat the same process with less scaffolding at A=(2,3.8).

Expected estimate: m≈3.2, giving y=3.2(x-2)+3.8.

Why this point: Neither coordinate is 0, so students actually use the full point-slope structure rather than a simplified special case.

Look for: Students noticing that a tangent line is a good local model near A but separates from the curve farther away.

Screen 7

Why not just set h = 0?

Student job: Move h all the way to 0 and observe the secant-slope fraction.

For f(x)=x² at x=1, the numerator and denominator both become 0, so the displayed secant fraction is 0/0, undefined.

Teacher move: Ask, “So why was getting close to 0 productive even though plugging in 0 fails?”

Desired idea: We can study what the secant slopes are approaching without needing a distinct second point at h=0.
Screen 8

Synthesis

Students should connect four ideas:

  1. As |h| shrinks, B approaches A.
  2. The secant line approaches a stable local direction.
  3. Nearby secant slopes can estimate the tangent slope.
  4. The tangent slope describes instantaneous change and gives a useful local linear model.

Common Misconceptions / Watch For

What students may say/doTeacher response
“h is the slope.”Return to the graph: h is horizontal change from A to B; the secant slope is calculated from the two points.
Only approaches from positive h.Ask them to test a similar negative h and compare the secant slopes.
Chooses a line that looks close globally.Ask them to zoom their attention near A. A tangent is a local match, not a line that follows the whole curve.
Writes y=mx+b and guesses b.Redirect to the fixed point and the displayed point-slope form.
Thinks h=0 should produce the tangent slope directly.Have them read numerator and denominator at h=0 and articulate why the secant construction requires two distinct points.
Uses “tangent touches once” as the definition.Do not reinforce it. The important idea here is the limiting local direction/rate of change.

Suggested Debrief

Keep the close centered on student observations rather than formal notation. A useful sequence is:

  1. “What stayed fixed in every secant experiment?”
  2. “What did h control?”
  3. “What happened to the secant slope as |h| became very small?”
  4. “Why was h=0 itself a problem?”
  5. “Why would scientists care about the slope of a tangent line?”
  6. “How did point-slope form let us turn that slope into an actual line?”
Successful first-day outcome: Students leave with an intuitive need for a limiting process and a concrete picture of tangent slope as instantaneous rate of change. They do not need formal limit notation, derivative notation, or derivative rules yet.
