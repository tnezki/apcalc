AP Calculus AB — Unit 1: Limits and Continuity

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Limits describe local and end behavior through graphical, numerical, and algebraic representations, and continuity connects those limits to function values and theorem-based conclusions.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment

Section Outline

Section 1.1: What Is a Limit?

Learning Target: Relate average versus instantaneous change to the idea of a limit and read and interpret limit notation.

I Can Statements

  • I can relate average versus instantaneous change to the idea of a limit and read and interpret limit notation.
  • I can estimate a limit from a graph or table.
  • I can connect shrinking secant intervals to instantaneous change.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • limit
  • average rate of change
  • instantaneous rate of change
  • limit notation

Key Representations

  • graphs approaching a point
  • tables of values
  • average-rate secant behavior
  • symbolic limit notation

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Treating the function value at a point as automatically equal to the limit.
  • Reading a limit as a value reached rather than a value approached.
  • Confusing average rate of change with instantaneous rate of change.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Estimate a limit from a graph or table.
  • Connect shrinking secant intervals to instantaneous change.

Spiral Planning

Current Focus: Relate average versus instantaneous change to the idea of a limit and read and interpret limit notation.

Last Focus: Prior knowledge on functions, graph interpretation, and rates of change, as named in the framework introduction to Section 1.1.

Mastery Priorities: Function notation; graph and table interpretation; algebraic manipulation; average rate of change.

Section 1.2: Limits from Graphs and Tables

Learning Target: Determine one-sided and two-sided limits from graphs and tables, including when a limit does not exist.

I Can Statements

  • I can determine one-sided and two-sided limits from graphs and tables, including when a limit does not exist.
  • I can compare graphical and numerical estimates of the same limit.
  • I can use calculator-supported tables as a check on graphical reasoning.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • left-hand limit
  • right-hand limit
  • two-sided limit
  • DNE

Key Representations

  • graphs with one-sided behavior
  • tables approaching from both sides
  • paired graphical and numerical representations

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Declaring DNE because the function is undefined at the point.
  • Ignoring disagreement between left- and right-hand limits.
  • Reading a table from only one side of the input.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Compare graphical and numerical estimates of the same limit.
  • Use calculator-supported tables as a check on graphical reasoning.

Spiral Planning

Current Focus: Determine one-sided and two-sided limits from graphs and tables, including when a limit does not exist.

Last Focus: Relate average versus instantaneous change to the idea of a limit and read and interpret limit notation.

Section 1.3: Algebraic Limits

Learning Target: Evaluate limits algebraically using direct substitution, limit laws, factoring, conjugates, and appropriate trigonometric or squeeze reasoning.

I Can Statements

  • I can evaluate limits algebraically using direct substitution, limit laws, factoring, conjugates, and appropriate trigonometric or squeeze reasoning.
  • I can select an algebraic method for a limit form.
  • I can compare two valid algebraic approaches to the same limit.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • direct substitution
  • limit laws
  • factoring
  • conjugate
  • Squeeze Theorem

Key Representations

  • symbolic expressions
  • factored forms
  • rationalized forms
  • trigonometric limit forms

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Stopping at an indeterminate form instead of simplifying.
  • Applying limit laws before checking whether their conditions support the operation.
  • Using factoring or conjugates without preserving equivalent expressions near the limit point.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Select an algebraic method for a limit form.
  • Compare two valid algebraic approaches to the same limit.

Spiral Planning

Current Focus: Evaluate limits algebraically using direct substitution, limit laws, factoring, conjugates, and appropriate trigonometric or squeeze reasoning.

Last Focus: Determine one-sided and two-sided limits from graphs and tables, including when a limit does not exist.

Mastery Priorities: Function notation; graph and table interpretation; algebraic manipulation; average rate of change.

Section 1.4: Continuity and Discontinuity

Learning Target: Analyze continuity at a point and on intervals, and classify discontinuities using limits, function values, and piecewise definitions.

I Can Statements

  • I can analyze continuity at a point and on intervals, and classify discontinuities using limits, function values, and piecewise definitions.
  • I can determine parameter values that make a piecewise function continuous.
  • I can justify a discontinuity classification from multiple representations.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • continuity
  • discontinuity
  • piecewise function
  • removable discontinuity
  • jump discontinuity

Key Representations

  • piecewise formulas
  • graphs with discontinuities
  • continuity condition statements
  • interval notation

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Checking only whether a function value exists rather than all continuity conditions.
  • Confusing a removable discontinuity with a jump or infinite discontinuity.
  • Forgetting to compare one-sided behavior at a piecewise boundary.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Determine parameter values that make a piecewise function continuous.
  • Justify a discontinuity classification from multiple representations.

Spiral Planning

Current Focus: Analyze continuity at a point and on intervals, and classify discontinuities using limits, function values, and piecewise definitions.

Last Focus: Evaluate limits algebraically using direct substitution, limit laws, factoring, conjugates, and appropriate trigonometric or squeeze reasoning.

Mastery Priorities: Function notation; graph and table interpretation; algebraic manipulation; average rate of change.

Section 1.5: Asymptotes and IVT

Learning Target: Interpret infinite limits and limits at infinity to identify asymptotic behavior, and apply the Intermediate Value Theorem when its hypotheses are satisfied.

I Can Statements

  • I can interpret infinite limits and limits at infinity to identify asymptotic behavior, and apply the Intermediate Value Theorem when its hypotheses are satisfied.
  • I can use asymptotic behavior to describe a function model.
  • I can determine whether IVT guarantees a value and justify the conclusion.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • infinite limit
  • vertical asymptote
  • limit at infinity
  • horizontal asymptote
  • Intermediate Value Theorem

Key Representations

  • graphs with asymptotes
  • end-behavior notation
  • theorem hypothesis/conclusion statements
  • interval representations

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Treating a vertical asymptote as a function value.
  • Assuming a horizontal asymptote cannot be crossed.
  • Using IVT without verifying continuity on the required interval.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Use asymptotic behavior to describe a function model.
  • Determine whether IVT guarantees a value and justify the conclusion.

Spiral Planning

Current Focus: Interpret infinite limits and limits at infinity to identify asymptotic behavior, and apply the Intermediate Value Theorem when its hypotheses are satisfied.

Last Focus: Analyze continuity at a point and on intervals, and classify discontinuities using limits, function values, and piecewise definitions.

Mastery Priorities: Function notation; graph and table interpretation; algebraic manipulation; average rate of change.

Unit Themes / Throughlines

Unit Themes / Throughlines
  • Move fluently among symbolic, graphical, and numerical descriptions of limiting behavior.
  • Use precise notation to distinguish function values, one-sided limits, two-sided limits, and infinite behavior.
  • Choose a representation or algebraic strategy based on the form of the limit.
  • Justify continuity and theorem use by checking required conditions.
  • Spiral interpretation, notation, and justification throughout limit work.

Assessment Methods by DOK Level

DOK 1 — Foundational Evidence

Format: brief proficiency checks, direct computation or reading, notation and recognition tasks.

Purpose: Gather evidence of foundational recall, recognition, notation, and direct execution in Unit 1.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Limit Notation and Reading Check Eligible After Section 1.1 — Read limit notation and identify direct graphical or numerical evidence for a stated limit. Evidence focus: Reveals whether notation and basic limit meaning are secure.
  2. One-Sided Limit Recognition Eligible After Section 1.2 — Identify left-hand, right-hand, and two-sided limits from a graph or short table. Evidence focus: Reveals whether students distinguish directional behavior.
  3. Algebraic Limit Fluency Eligible After Section 1.3 — Carry out direct substitution or a clearly indicated algebraic simplification for routine limits. Evidence focus: Reveals foundational procedural accuracy.
  4. Continuity and Asymptote Vocabulary Check Eligible After Section 1.5 — Recognize continuity conditions, discontinuity types, and asymptotic features from concise representations. Evidence focus: Reveals whether essential terminology is linked to the correct features.
What this tells the teacher:
Whether students are demonstrating the intended DOK 1 evidence for Unit 1 without promoting routine work to a higher cognitive level.
DOK 2 — Application Evidence

Format: multi-representation application, routine method selection, applied computation, and interpretation.

Purpose: Gather evidence of routine application and translation among unit representations in Unit 1.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Graph-Table Limit Match Eligible After Section 1.2 — Match a graph and table that describe the same limiting behavior and explain the routine connection. Evidence focus: Shows whether students can translate between graphical and numerical representations.
  2. Method Selection for Algebraic Limits Eligible After Section 1.3 — Choose and carry out an appropriate routine method such as factoring or conjugates for several limit forms. Evidence focus: Shows whether students can connect form to method.
  3. Piecewise Continuity Application Eligible After Section 1.4 — Use limits and function values to determine continuity at a piecewise boundary. Evidence focus: Shows whether students can coordinate the continuity conditions.
  4. Asymptote and IVT Application Eligible After Section 1.5 — Interpret asymptotic behavior and determine whether IVT applies in a stated situation with explicit conditions. Evidence focus: Shows whether students can apply unit concepts to routine theorem and behavior questions.
What this tells the teacher:
Whether students are demonstrating the intended DOK 2 evidence for Unit 1 without promoting routine work to a higher cognitive level.
DOK 3 — Strategic / Reasoning Evidence

Format: constructed response, method comparison, critique, theorem/condition reasoning, and representation synthesis.

Purpose: Gather evidence of strategic reasoning, justification, and non-routine method selection in Unit 1.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Competing Limit Strategies Eligible After Section 1.3 — Compare two plausible approaches to a non-routine limit and justify which is valid and efficient. Evidence focus: Shows strategic method selection rather than routine execution.
  2. Continuity Claim Critique Eligible After Section 1.4 — Evaluate a student claim about continuity or a discontinuity classification using multiple pieces of evidence. Evidence focus: Shows whether students can critique reasoning and connect conditions.
  3. IVT Hypothesis Justification Eligible After Section 1.5 — Decide whether a theorem-based conclusion is guaranteed when conditions are partially implicit, and justify the decision. Evidence focus: Shows theorem-condition reasoning.
  4. Representation Synthesis for Limits Eligible After Section 1.5 — Reconcile graphical, numerical, and symbolic information when the representations appear to conflict. Evidence focus: Shows strategic synthesis across representations.
What this tells the teacher:
Whether students are demonstrating the intended DOK 3 evidence for Unit 1 without promoting routine work to a higher cognitive level.
DOK 4 — Performance Evidence

Format: modeling performance tasks, investigations, multi-condition analysis, synthesis, and critique-and-revise work.

Purpose: Gather evidence of extended performance, modeling, transfer, synthesis, and revision in Unit 1.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Limit Evidence Investigation Eligible After Section 1.2 — Collect or generate numerical and graphical evidence around a point, propose a limit, and revise the conclusion if representations disagree. Evidence focus: Shows extended evidence use and revision.
  2. Build a Continuity Model Eligible After Section 1.4 — Construct a piecewise function model meeting specified continuity and discontinuity conditions, test it, and document revisions. Evidence focus: Shows performance-level modeling with constraints.
  3. Asymptotic Behavior Design Task Eligible After Section 1.5 — Design and analyze a function representation with specified vertical and horizontal asymptotic behavior across multiple conditions. Evidence focus: Shows transfer of asymptote concepts into model construction.
  4. Theorem Decision Performance Eligible After Section 1.5 — Analyze a set of function scenarios, select where IVT can support a conclusion, and revise unsupported claims with evidence. Evidence focus: Shows extended theorem reasoning across cases.
What this tells the teacher:
Whether students are demonstrating the intended DOK 4 evidence for Unit 1 without promoting routine work to a higher cognitive level.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 1 Summative Assessment Eligible after Section 1.5, with timing adjusted to the framework pacing and instructional needs.

Recommended format: A course-appropriate AP-style mixed assessment using selected-response and constructed-response/FRQ-style evidence, with calculator or no-calculator conditions when the relevant framework section calls for them. The plan does not prescribe a fixed paper-only length.

Major evidence priorities: limits from graphs, tables, and algebraic forms; continuity and discontinuity; asymptotic behavior; and theorem-based reasoning with IVT.

Representation / reasoning / interpretation expectations: Students should work across the representations emphasized in the unit, use precise notation, justify conclusions when conditions matter, and include units or contextual interpretation when the framework makes them relevant.

Conventional summative questions should use DOK levels that naturally fit the assessed content. DOK 4 evidence is normally collected through performance-oriented modeling, investigation, design, synthesis, or transfer tasks rather than forced into ordinary test questions.

Spiral / retention statement: Include deliberate retrieval of earlier prerequisite ideas that remain active in the unit, especially notation, representation interpretation, and previously established calculus relationships; do not treat spiral work as random review.

Holistic Rubric

A — Highly Proficient

The student independently analyzes limit and continuity behavior across graphical, numerical, and algebraic representations and justifies theorem-based conclusions. Work is accurate, well-organized, and supported by clear reasoning. The student connects relevant representations, concepts, evidence, or models and communicates conclusions with precision and independence.

B — Proficient

The student generally demonstrates proficiency with limits from graphs, tables, and algebraic forms; continuity and discontinuity; asymptotic behavior; and theorem-based reasoning with IVT. Most work is accurate, and the student can complete standard and moderately complex tasks independently. Reasoning and communication are generally clear, though they may lack some precision, flexibility, or depth.

C — Developing Proficiency

The student demonstrates developing proficiency with limits from graphs, tables, and algebraic forms; continuity and discontinuity; asymptotic behavior; and theorem-based reasoning with IVT. The student can complete many routine tasks but may need support with reasoning, representations, notation, evidence, modeling, or multi-step problem solving. Work may contain errors or incomplete explanations, but there is sufficient evidence of developing understanding of the central ideas.

Not Yet — Insufficient Evidence

The student does not yet demonstrate consistent proficiency with limits from graphs, tables, and algebraic forms; continuity and discontinuity; asymptotic behavior; and theorem-based reasoning with IVT. Work shows major misconceptions, significant procedural or conceptual errors, weak reasoning, or difficulty connecting the representations, evidence, or models required by the unit. Additional instruction, practice, feedback, or reassessment may be needed before proficiency is demonstrated.
